# ITSM Roles & Career

ITSM (IT service management) is the discipline of organizing how IT services are delivered, supported, and improved — and every company running IT services needs people who can do that work. That creates demand beyond pure security or development roles, with clear entry points even for career changers. Job titles, tooling, and certification paths vary and change — verify current details on peoplecert.org and local job listings.

## Why ITSM is a career path

Technology alone doesn't deliver value; someone has to design, run, and improve how it's used. ITSM roles give you a structured career inside almost any industry — banking, government, healthcare, retail — because the need for well-run IT services is not tied to one sector.

## Roles

Titles vary significantly by organization — treat these as typical shapes, not fixed definitions.

| Role | What they do | Typical entry point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service Desk / Support Analyst | First contact for users; restores service and handles requests | Support, help desk, or service desk |
| Incident Manager | Coordinates major incidents and drives restoration | Senior service desk or operations |
| Problem Manager | Finds root cause and drives prevention of repeat incidents | Incident or service desk role |
| Change Manager | Authorizes and schedules changes to reduce risk | Operations or project coordination |
| Service Level Manager | Owns customer agreements and service reviews | Service desk or account-facing roles |
| Service Owner | Accountable for one service end to end | Senior analyst or process specialist |
| ITSM Process Owner / Practitioner | Designs and improves a specific practice | Analyst with process experience |
| ITSM Consultant / Architect | Designs the whole service management setup | Senior delivery or consulting roles |

## Career progression

A typical (not universal) shape:

```text
Analyst → Senior analyst → Team lead / process specialist → Service manager → Head of ITSM / CIO
```

Paths vary: some people move laterally into problem or change roles, others specialize in tooling or consulting. Certifications (Foundation first, then higher modules — verify current paths on peoplecert.org) signal progression but never replace examples of work you've actually done.

## Skills that matter

| Skill | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Clear communication | Users under stress need calm, precise answers |
| Empathy with users | Service quality is experienced, not documented |
| Structured thinking | Incidents and problems need orderly diagnosis |
| Willingness to document | If it isn't written down, it doesn't scale |
| Basic technical breadth | Enough to diagnose and route issues correctly |
| Curiosity about the business | Value is defined by the business, not IT |
| Tooling familiarity | Common tooling includes ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, Freshservice — name them only as examples; verify the current landscape in your target market |

## How ITIL fits

ITIL gives you the shared language you'll hear in interviews: incident vs. problem vs. request, service level agreements, the service value chain. Foundation certification signals that baseline knowledge to employers. But interviews are won with experience and concrete examples — pair the certification with real stories of services you've supported or processes you've improved.

## Complementary knowledge

| Area | One-liner |
| --- | --- |
| Basic networking & OS concepts | Enough to triage: DNS, ports, services, logs |
| Security fundamentals | Protecting services is part of the job — see the suite's security sites, e.g. [Information Security Management](../practices/information-security-management.md) |
| Project/agile basics | Scrum and Kanban vocabulary shows up in improvement work |
| Data & reporting basics | You'll be asked for SLA attainment, backlog trends, and simple charts |

## Breaking in without experience

- Volunteer to run a ticket queue at your current job, even informally.
- Document your team's processes — writing them down is process experience.
- Build a home lab with a ticketing tool and run a fake service desk.
- Translate any customer-facing experience (retail, support, hospitality) into service desk skills: triage, empathy, follow-up.

## First 90 days on a service desk

| Phase | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| Days 1-30 | Learn the tools, escalation paths, and top request types; shadow experienced analysts |
| Days 31-60 | Take independent tickets; start writing short knowledge articles for repeat issues |
| Days 61-90 | Handle escalations calmly; identify one recurring pain point worth improving |

## Interview preparation

| Prepare | How |
| --- | --- |
| A STAR example of restoring a broken service | Situation, task, action, result — with your actual actions |
| Incident vs. problem vs. request | Explain each clearly with a one-line example |
| A time you improved a process | Before/after, what you changed, what happened |

### Questions you should be able to answer

- Walk me through what happens when a user reports a service is down.
- What is the difference between an incident and a problem, and why keep them separate?
- How would you explain an SLA breach to a non-technical stakeholder?
- Tell me about a time you handled an upset customer and what you did.

## Day-to-day reality

Expect a mix of repetitive work and sudden urgency. A typical week contains triage, handoffs, documentation upkeep, and one or two situations that need calm coordination. The people who last are the ones who document as they go instead of waiting for a quiet moment that rarely comes.

## Salary note

Compensation varies hugely by country, company, and experience. Check current local job listings rather than trusting published averages — and be wary of any single number presented without context.
